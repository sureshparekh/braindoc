What BRAIN is#

BRAIN fits the spectrum of a galaxy, pixel by pixel, with a mixture of stellar populations, dust, kinematics and ionised gas, and reports what that mixture says about the galaxy: how old its stars are, how much metal they hold, how much dust they sit behind, how fast they move, and what the gas between them is doing.

It does this for a single spectrum, for a table of thousands of spectra, and for every spaxel of an integral-field (IFU) datacube, on a laptop CPU or on one or more GPUs. The Python package is called brainsp; it installs a desktop interface, a command-line tool and a library, all three driving the same engine with the same settings.

The path every fit takes. You provide step 1 and choose what happens in step 2; BRAIN does steps 3 and 4; step 5 is yours, in Python or in the interface.

What it measures#

From a spectrum, or from each spaxel of a datacube, BRAIN measures:

  • the stellar populations: the share of light and of mass in every simple stellar population (SSP) of the template library, and from them the light- and mass-weighted mean age and metallicity, the star formation history, and the stellar mass when a distance is given;

  • the dust: the extinction \(A_V\) of the whole population, and an extra extinction \(Y_V\) for the youngest stars, with the extinction law of your choice;

  • the stellar kinematics: the line-of-sight velocity \(v_\star\) and dispersion \(\sigma_\star\), corrected for the instrument and the template library’s own resolution where both are known;

  • the emission lines (espop and nespop modes): the flux, equivalent width, velocity and dispersion of every line in its catalogue (70 optical and 28 near-infrared lines), the Balmer decrement and the BPT and WHAN classes;

  • the nebular emission (nespop mode): the nebular continuum that the young stars’ ionising photons produce, with H\(\alpha\) and H\(\beta\) tied to the same photons, as in FADO;

  • a 1σ uncertainty for every one of these, either from the fit’s own covariance or from a bootstrap of the whole fit.

The three fitting modes explains the three modes, and Key concepts the words used here.

The three share one set of settings (83 of them, every one with a sensible default), so a run set up in one can be repeated in another. The interface can save its run as a Python file, and a megacube written from Python opens in the interface’s viewer.

What you need#

  • Python 3.10 or newer, on Linux, macOS or Windows;

  • for the fit itself, nothing else: JAX, NumPy, SciPy, Astropy and Matplotlib are installed with BRAIN;

  • for the interface, a display (Qt is installed with BRAIN);

  • optionally, an NVIDIA GPU with a recent driver, which makes datacubes several times faster but changes no number.

Installation has the details, and Check your installation checks the result.
