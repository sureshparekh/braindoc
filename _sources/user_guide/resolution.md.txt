# Instrumental resolution

A spectrograph smears every spectral line by its line-spread function (LSF),
and so did whatever produced the template library. The two are almost never
equal, and the difference goes straight onto the velocity dispersion BRAIN
reports. Tell BRAIN both and it corrects for the difference and reports an
intrinsic stellar dispersion; leave them blank and it still fits, but the
dispersion it reports is an *observed* one, which includes the instrument.

| Setting | Means | Default |
|---|---|---|
| `instrumental_resolution` | the data's LSF, in the **observed** frame | blank |
| `library_resolution` | the library's LSF, at **rest** wavelengths | read from the template headers |
| `spectrum_redshift` | the redshift removed from the spectrum | 0 |

## Writing a resolution

```yaml
instrumental_resolution: 2.9             # a FWHM in Angstrom
instrumental_resolution: "70 km/s"       # constant in velocity, a 1-sigma width
instrumental_resolution: lsf_10517.txt   # a file: wavelength, FWHM (Angstrom)
```

A string must carry a unit: `"2.51"` is refused rather than guessed, because
Ångström and km/s differ by a factor of about twenty in the answer. A plain
number is a FWHM in Ångström. `"45 km/s"` is a **1-sigma** width; spectrograph
manuals usually quote a FWHM, and reading one as the other changes the
variance by a factor of 5.55, so the log says which was used.

MaNGA cubes ship the LSF per spaxel, as a 1-sigma width in Ångström:
`LSFPRE` and `LSFPOST` in DR17 (`PREDISP` and `DISP` in DR15 and DR16). Take
the pre-pixelised one, `LSFPRE`, the convention that matches this kind of
fitting.

## What BRAIN does with them

It convolves the **templates** up to the data's resolution, once, at load
time, on each template's own wavelength grid, before mapping them onto the
fitting grid. It never smooths the spectrum: every $\chi^2$ here assumes
independent pixel noise, and smoothing correlates it, so a smoothed spectrum
would give a better-looking fit that means nothing.

The decision is taken separately for each group of templates sharing a
stated resolution, over the pixels actually fitted. BasesGM is mixed (56
templates at 2.51 Å FWHM, 28 at 0.3 Å FWHM plus 45 km/s), and a group sharper
than the data is broadened while a group broader than the data somewhere in
the window is left alone; the summary counts each.

The data's resolution is applied at the observed wavelength,
$\lambda_{\rm rest}(1+z)$, with $z$ the redshift that was removed. If any
template states no resolution and `library_resolution` is not set, no
correction is applied and the intrinsic dispersion is reported as `unknown`
rather than averaged over the templates that do state one.

## In the results

| | |
|---|---|
| `vd_min`, `sigma_obs` | the dispersion the fit applied to the library: **observed** |
| `delta_sigma_inst` | the signed quadrature difference of the two LSFs (data minus library) |
| `sigma_star` | $\mathrm{sign}(x)\sqrt{|x|}$, $x = \sigma_{\rm obs}^2 - \Delta\sigma_{\rm inst}^2$: **intrinsic** |
| `FitResult.sigma`, `.sigma_star` | the same, in Python |
| megacube `Sigma_star` / `Sigma_star_c` | observed / corrected maps, with `LSF_flag` |

A `sigma_star` written with a leading `<` is an upper limit: the fit sat on
the lower bound of `dispersion_range`, or the LSF difference alone exceeds
what the fit applied. The gas dispersions are always *observed*; `sigma_inst`
is printed beside each so the subtraction is available, as in Westfall et al.
(2019).

## Degrading the data instead

The datacube pipeline can also smooth the data to a coarser resolution
(`degrade_resolution`), to reproduce an older analysis. It
correlates the noise and warns every time it runs, and using it together with
`instrumental_resolution` is refused: that would count the instrument twice.
