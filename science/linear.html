The linear solve#

At fixed non-linear parameters \(\boldsymbol\theta\), the model is linear in the weights: \(M = A(\boldsymbol\theta)\,\mathbf w\), where column \(j\) of \(A\) is template \(j\) broadened, shifted and attenuated (and, in the line modes, a column per emission line; in nespop, the nebular continuum inside each population’s column). The weights solve

\[ \min_{\mathbf w \ge 0}\; \lVert W^{1/2}(A\mathbf w - \mathbf f)\rVert^2 + \lVert D\,\mathbf w_\star\rVert^2, \qquad W = \mathrm{diag}(m_i/\sigma_i^2), \]

a non-negative least-squares problem on the augmented system

\[\begin{split} \tilde A = \begin{pmatrix} W^{1/2}A \\ D\;\;0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \tilde{\mathbf f} = \begin{pmatrix} W^{1/2}\mathbf f \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}, \end{split}\]

with, in nespop, the two Case B rows appended (The nebular emission).

Exact non-negative least squares#

BRAIN solves it exactly, with the Lawson–Hanson active-set method: the weights move between an active set (held at zero) and a passive set (solved by unconstrained least squares) until the Karush–Kuhn–Tucker conditions hold. It reaches SciPy’s NNLS optimum to about \(10^{-12}\). It is written in JAX with fixed-size loops, so that it compiles and runs for many spaxels at once, and each solve starts from the previous one’s active set, which gives the same answer in fewer iterations.

The solution is sparse: typically 5 to 20 of 84 templates carry light. That is the honest answer of a degenerate problem, not a failure to converge, and the reason to report averages over templates rather than single weights.

Double precision#

\(\tilde A^{\sf T}\tilde A\) has condition numbers near \(10^{10}\), because templates of neighbouring age and metallicity are nearly collinear, so the solve needs double precision; in single precision the weights change. BRAIN computes in double precision everywhere, on the CPU and on GPUs alike.

Regularisation#

smoothness \(\lambda_s\) (default 0, off) adds a Tikhonov penalty on the stellar weights, \(\lVert D\mathbf w_\star\rVert^2\), with \(D\) the first- or second-difference operator (smoothness_order):

\[ (D_1\mathbf w)_j = \lambda_s(w_{j+1} - w_j), \qquad (D_2\mathbf w)_j = \lambda_s(w_{j+2} - 2w_{j+1} + w_j), \]

over the templates in catalogue order (by metallicity, then by age). It favours star formation histories that vary smoothly with age, and so biases them towards smoothness by design; use it to stabilise histories at low S/N, and report it.

The simplex constraint#

enforce_simplex: true adds \(\sum_{j\le N_\star} w_j = 1\) to the system. It is off by default: the constraint is only right together with a fitted normalisation, and the light fractions are normalised to 100 per cent either way.
